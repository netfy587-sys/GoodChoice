beauty, before brands
Traditional rituals from around the world

Long before the beauty aisle, there were kitchens, gardens, and grandmothers.

Most of what a modern beauty counter sells has a much older ancestor — a paste, a powder, or an oil that a community relied on for generations. This is a look at five such traditions, still practiced today, without a single brand attached to any of them.

South Asia

Turmeric: the golden paste before every celebration

In South Asian households, an "ubtan" — turmeric mixed with gram flour, milk, or yogurt — has been applied before weddings and festivals for generations, valued for leaving skin visibly brighter and smoother.

  • Turmeric has documented anti-inflammatory and antibacterial properties, which is part of why it was traditionally used on both skin and minor wounds.
  • The pre-wedding "haldi" ceremony turns the ritual into a shared, communal event rather than a private routine.
  • Modern skincare's interest in turmeric-based formulas traces directly back to this centuries-old household practice.
South Asia · Middle East · North Africa

Henna: decoration with a cooling purpose

Ground from the leaves of the henna plant, this paste has marked weddings, festivals, and rites of passage across multiple, largely unconnected cultures for thousands of years.

  • Beyond decoration, henna paste was traditionally valued for its cooling effect on skin in hot climates.
  • Bridal henna ceremonies exist independently in South Asian, Middle Eastern, and North African traditions, each with distinct patterns and meanings.
  • The stain fades naturally over one to three weeks, requiring no removal product of any kind.
West Africa

Shea butter: a whole household craft

Extracted from the nut of the African shea tree, this ivory-colored fat has been produced by West African communities — largely by women, passing the process down through families — for centuries.

  • Its high fatty-acid content makes it especially effective at softening dry, cracked skin.
  • Traditional processing involves roasting, grinding, and hand-whipping the nut paste over several days.
  • The cooperative, community-based production of shea butter remains an important source of income for many West African women today.
East Asia

Rice water: the starchy rinse behind famously long hair

Communities across China and Japan have used the water left over from washing or fermenting rice as a hair rinse and skin toner for centuries — most famously among the Yao women of Huangluo village, known for hair that often reaches the floor.

  • Rice water contains inositol, a carbohydrate that can coat hair strands and reduce friction damage.
  • As a skin toner, it's traditionally used for a mild brightening and smoothing effect.
  • The practice requires nothing beyond rice and water, which is part of why it has persisted for so long.
Ancient Egypt · Middle East · South Asia

Kohl: humanity's oldest cosmetic with a practical job

Worn by both men and women in ancient Egypt, kohl was ground from minerals into a fine powder and lined along the eyes — a practice believed to have started long before it became decorative.

  • Beyond appearance, ancient wearers associated kohl with reducing glare from the sun and warding off eye infections.
  • The same basic technique — a powder applied along the lash line with a stick — evolved independently into today's kajal, surma, and eyeliner traditions.
  • Kohl containers recovered from Egyptian tombs are among the oldest cosmetic artifacts ever found.

These traditions still hold their own

Many modern skincare and makeup ingredients — brightening actives, plant oils, natural dyes, mineral pigments — are, in some form, a return to ingredients these traditions never stopped using. The names on the label changed far more than the ingredients themselves.

Not every good idea in beauty is new. Some of the oldest ones just got rediscovered.

Each of these practices grew out of what a specific community had on hand — a plant, a grain, a mineral — refined over generations without a single advertisement. That's worth remembering the next time a "new" ingredient shows up with a modern name.
